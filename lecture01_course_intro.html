Optimization: Taking Optimal Decisions

In complex systems, "common sense" strategies can be misleading.

Optimization provides a principled way to choose the best action, subject to constraints and trade-offs.

Every optimization problem has three ingredients:
  • Decisions — what we control
  • Constraints — what limits us
  • Objective — what "best" means

Our job this semester: make these notions precise — and computable.

Optimization in the wild

Where Optimization Runs the World

Airlines — which crew flies which trip, which aircraft serves which route, how tickets are priced. Savings: billions per year.
Energy — which generators run each hour and at what level. U.S. power markets re-solve this every five minutes.
Logistics — delivery routes, warehouse locations, how to pack containers. Every package you receive rode an optimized route.
Manufacturing — production mixes, cutting patterns that waste less material, blending recipes for fuel and food.
Finance — portfolios trading off return against risk; spotting arbitrage before it disappears.
Healthcare — nurse schedules, radiation therapy plans, matching kidney donors to patients.
Sports — Major League Baseball’s season schedule is built with integer programming.
Machine learning — every training run is an optimization problem: minimize a loss function.

Let’s look inside three of these — in words, no equations needed yet.

Spotlight 1 · continuous & linear

Radiation Therapy Planning

A tumor is hit by beams from many angles. Each beam is split into thousands of tiny beamlets whose intensities we can set individually. Dose received is proportional to intensity, and doses from different beamlets add up.

Model in words:
choose — an intensity for every beamlet
minimize — total dose hitting healthy tissue
subject to — tumor gets at least the prescribed dose; each organ-at-risk stays under its safety limit; intensities can’t be negative
Flavor: LINEAR. Proportional + additive = our axioms! An LP with ~100,000 variables, solved in seconds — clinics run these daily.
Spotlight 2 · discrete decisions

Airline Crew Scheduling

Thousands of flights must each get a crew. Crews fly multi-day trips called pairings that must obey rest rules and end back at the crew’s home base.

Model in words:
choose — yes/no for every candidate pairing
minimize — total crew cost
subject to — every flight covered by exactly one chosen pairing; work & rest rules respected
Flavor: DISCRETE. You can’t fly 0.7 of a trip — decisions are yes/no. That breaks the divisibility axiom: an integer program with millions of binary variables.
How is it actually solved? Branch-and-bound: a search tree where every node solves an LP relaxation. LP does the heavy lifting.
Spotlight 3 · smooth & nonlinear

Portfolio Selection

Split your wealth among assets. Expected return is linear in your allocations — but risk isn’t: variance involves products of allocations (assets move together!), so risk is a quadratic function.

Model in words:
choose — the fraction of wealth in each asset
maximize — expected return, minus a penalty for risk
subject to — fractions sum to 1; position limits
Flavor: NONLINEAR. Products of variables break the additivity axiom — the objective curves. (Markowitz won a Nobel Prize for this model.)
LP sneaks in anyway: swap variance for mean-absolute-deviation risk and the model becomes an LP — and nonlinear algorithms navigate by local linear models (gradients).
Where this course sits

The Decision-Making Maturity Curve

Framing popularized by David Simchi-Levi (MIT): each level consumes the ones below it. Optimization turns data, statistics, and predictions into decisions.
A dose of realism

You Can't Walk In and Start Optimizing

Imagine day one at a hospital: "I'm here to optimize your nurse schedules." What has to exist before that sentence makes sense?

1. The data — shift records, demand by hour, absence rates. No data, no model: every coefficient \(a_{ij}, b_i, c_j\) has to come from somewhere.
2. The statistics & predictions — demand forecasts, no-show probabilities, length-of-stay estimates. Optimization consumes predictions; garbage in, confidently-optimal garbage out.
3. Organizational readiness — processes stable enough to model, and people actually willing to act on a model's recommendation. A perfect schedule nobody follows optimizes nothing.
Some places aren't there yet — they need systems engineering, process design, and data infrastructure first. Optimization has its place on the maturity curve; it can't be forced onto every organization at every stage.
As ISEs, you'll often build the lower rungs — data pipelines, statistics, forecasts, process improvements — before the optimization step even becomes possible. That's not a detour; it's the job.

A First LP: Production Planning

A shop makes two products, \(A\) and \(B\):
Product AProduct BAvailable
Profit / unit$40$55—
Labor (hrs / unit)45200
Material (kg / unit)24160
Formulation \[ \begin{aligned} \max\quad & 40x_A + 55x_B \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & 4x_A + 5x_B \le 200 \;\;\text{(labor)}\\ & 2x_A + 4x_B \le 160 \;\;\text{(material)}\\ & x_A,\, x_B \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Matrix form \[ \max\{\, c^\top x \;:\; Ax \le b,\; x \ge 0 \,\} \] \[ c=\begin{bmatrix}40\\55\end{bmatrix},\; A=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\2&4\end{bmatrix},\; b=\begin{bmatrix}200\\160\end{bmatrix} \]

Preview: The Geometry of an LP

Each constraint cuts the plane in half. What survives is the feasible region.

Slide the profit line \(40x_A+55x_B = t\):

profit t = $600

Push the line as far as it will go… the last point it touches is a corner of the region.

Surprise: the best plan makes zero units of A! Intuition alone wouldn't tell you that.

Mathematical Programs

General form \[ \begin{aligned} \min / \max \quad & f(x_1,\dots,x_n)\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & g_i(x_1,\dots,x_n) \;\{\le,\ge,=\}\; b_i, \quad i=1,\dots,m\\ & x_j \ge 0,\quad j = 1,\dots,n \end{aligned} \]
  • Decision variables \(x_j\) — things we control
  • Constraints — structural, operational, technological limits
  • Objective function \(f\) — the criterion to optimize
  • Feasible solution — an \(x\) satisfying all constraints
In this course: the special case where \(f\) and every \(g_i\) are linear → Linear Programming (LP).

LP Standard Form

\[ \begin{array}{rll} \min & z = c\,x & \text{(objective)}\\[2px] \text{s.t.} & Ax = b & \text{(structural constraints)}\\[2px] & x \ge 0 & \text{(non-negativity)} \end{array} \]
Feasible region: \(\{x : Ax=b,\; x\ge 0\}\)
Optimal solution: feasible \(x^*\) with \(c\,x^* \le c\,\hat{x}\) for every feasible \(\hat{x}\)
\[ x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n\end{bmatrix}\; b=\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\ \vdots\\ b_m\end{bmatrix}\; c=\begin{bmatrix}c_1 & \cdots & c_n\end{bmatrix} \] \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&\cdots&a_{1n}\\ \vdots& &\vdots\\ a_{m1}&\cdots&a_{mn}\end{bmatrix} \]
\(A,b,c\) are known data; we must find \(x\).

The Axioms of Linear Programming

  1. Proportionality — contribution is proportional to activity level: \(c_j x_j\), \(a_{ij}x_j\)
  2. Additivity — effects add up; no cross terms like \(x_1 x_3\)
    (1) + (2) ⇒ Linearity
  3. Divisibility — fractional values allowed: \(x_j = 2.32\) is fine
  4. Certainty — all data \(A, b, c\) known exactly
Relax an axiom → new field:
  • drop (1)+(2) → Nonlinear Programming
  • drop (3) → Integer Programming (ISE 5406 & beyond)
  • drop (4) → Stochastic Programming
LP is the backbone — the other fields build on it.
One picture · three flavors

Linear · Mixed-Integer · Nonlinear

Linear: flat sides, flat costs — an optimum sits at a corner.
Mixed-integer: only lattice points allowed.
Nonlinear: curved boundary & costs.
Punchline: drop integrality and a MIP becomes an LP (its relaxation — an optimistic bound). Tangent lines turn curved regions into LPs too. LP is the common denominator.
Why study LP so deeply?

LP is the Master Key

Linear
Programming
geometry · simplex · duality
→ Integer programming
branch-and-bound = a search tree where every node is an LP relaxation
→ Nonlinear optimization
algorithms navigate by local linear models — gradients, cutting planes, Frank–Wolfe
→ Network flows
shortest paths, max flow, assignment: LPs with special structure, solved blazingly fast
→ Duality & prices
shadow prices, sensitivity, market design — the economics inside every model
Master LP and you hold the key to all of it — that’s why this course goes deep instead of wide.

The Modeling Workflow

  1. Problem analysis — understand objectives and constraints
  2. Modeling — translate relationships into math
  3. Model analysis — feasibility, convexity, structure
  4. Solution methods — pick algorithms, compute solutions
  5. Validation — check assumptions, sensitivity, iterate
This loop repeats: real modeling is iterative, not one-shot.

What We Will Learn to Do

LP in general form \[ \begin{aligned} \min\ & c^\top x\\ \text{s.t.}\ & Ax \le b,\; A_{=}x = b_{=},\; A_{\ge}x \ge b_{\ge} \end{aligned} \]
Linear objective + linear constraints; solvable at enormous scale.
  • Simplex family — walks corner to corner; fast in practice
  • Duality & optimality — certificates that a solution is best, plus sensitivity insight
  • Modern solvers (Gurobi) — simplex + barrier + presolve, at industrial scale
Theory ↔ computation: we prove why algorithms work, and use them on real models in Python.

    Airlines re-plan constantly: crews, aircraft, prices. Crew scheduling alone involves millions of yes/no choices.

    Decisions
    • Which crew pairing to fly (yes/no each)
    • Which aircraft type serves each route
    • How many seats to sell at each fare
    Constraints
    • Every flight covered by exactly one crew
    • FAA duty & rest rules; return to base
    • Fleet counts, maintenance windows, gates
    Objectives
    • Minimize crew + fuel cost
    • Maximize ticket revenue
    • Minimize delay propagation
    Flavor: integer programming at massive scale — solved by branch-and-bound over LP relaxations.
    Data needed first: full flight schedule, crew cost rules, historical demand by route & fare class, disruption statistics.

    Grid operators decide which plants run and at what level — a fresh optimization every few minutes, national scale.

    Decisions
    • Unit on/off status each hour (discrete)
    • Output level of each running unit (continuous)
    • Reserve capacity held back
    Constraints
    • Supply = demand, every moment, everywhere
    • Ramp-rate limits; min up/down times
    • Transmission line capacities
    Objectives
    • Minimize fuel + startup cost
    • Minimize emissions (or price them)
    Flavor: unit commitment = MIP; dispatch = LP; true AC power physics = nonlinear (usually linearized!).
    Data needed first: demand forecasts (ML!), generator cost curves, outage statistics, network parameters.

    Every package rides a chain of optimized decisions: which warehouse, which truck, which route, which order of stops.

    Decisions
    • Route and stop sequence per vehicle
    • Package-to-vehicle assignment
    • Where to place warehouses (long-run)
    Constraints
    • Vehicle capacity; driver hour limits
    • Delivery time windows
    • Every package delivered exactly once
    Objectives
    • Minimize miles / fuel / cost
    • Maximize on-time percentage
    Flavor: vehicle routing = hard combinatorial IP; heavily attacked with LP bounds + heuristics.
    Data needed first: demand by location, travel-time predictions, cost models, service-level statistics.

    The original LP applications: what to make, how to cut, what to blend — the production examples in this course.

    Decisions
    • Quantity of each product
    • Which cutting patterns to use
    • Blend fractions (fuels, feeds, alloys)
    Constraints
    • Machine & labor capacity
    • Raw material availability
    • Quality/recipe specifications, demand
    Objectives
    • Maximize profit
    • Minimize waste or cost
    Flavor: mostly genuinely linear — proportional, additive, divisible. LP's home turf since the 1940s.
    Data needed first: bills of materials, capacity measurements, cost accounting, demand forecasts.

    Allocate wealth across assets balancing return against risk — Markowitz's Nobel-winning model and its LP cousins.

    Decisions
    • Fraction of wealth per asset
    • Trades to rebalance a portfolio
    Constraints
    • Budget: fractions sum to 1
    • Position limits, no-short rules
    • Regulatory / risk limits
    Objectives
    • Maximize return − risk penalty
    • Minimize tracking error vs an index
    Flavor: variance risk = quadratic (nonlinear); absolute-deviation risk versions are LPs; arbitrage detection is pure LP duality (later in this course!).
    Data needed first: return histories, covariance estimates (statistics!), transaction cost models.

    From radiation dose maps to staff schedules to kidney exchanges — optimization with lives in the objective function.

    Decisions
    • Beamlet intensities (continuous)
    • Nurse-to-shift assignments (discrete)
    • Donor–patient matches (discrete)
    Constraints
    • Tumor dose minimums, organ dose caps
    • Shift coverage, work rules
    • Medical compatibility, exchange cycles
    Objectives
    • Minimize dose to healthy tissue
    • Minimize staffing cost / overtime
    • Maximize number of transplants
    Flavor: therapy planning ≈ LP; scheduling & matching = IP.
    Data needed first: imaging & dose physics, patient arrival statistics, compatibility testing — and clinical workflows ready to use the answer.

    Season schedules juggle venues, travel, TV and fairness — Major League Baseball's is produced with integer programming.

    Decisions
    • Which teams play where, each day
    • Series groupings and off days
    Constraints
    • Venue availability
    • Travel distance / back-to-back limits
    • Broadcast windows, rivalry dates, fairness
    Objectives
    • Minimize total travel
    • Maximize attractive matchups in prime slots
    Flavor: pure integer programming — schedules don't come in fractions.
    Data needed first: venue calendars, travel times, broadcast contracts, historical attendance.

    Training any model is optimization: choose parameters minimizing a loss. The fields are siblings, not rivals.

    Decisions
    • Model weights (millions–billions of them)
    Constraints
    • Often none explicit — or regularization playing that role
    Objectives
    • Minimize prediction loss on data
    Flavor: high-dimensional nonlinear (gradient methods — ISE 5406). But LP appears inside ML too: \(\ell_1\)/robust regression, optimal transport.
    Data needed first: the training data is the model — and ML's outputs (forecasts) are optimization's inputs, one rung down the maturity curve.
