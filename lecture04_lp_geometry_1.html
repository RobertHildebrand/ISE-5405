The General Optimization Problem

\[\min\; f(x) \quad \text{s.t.}\; x\in S\]
with \(f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) and \(S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\).
Trichotomy of outcomes:
  • infeasible — \(S=\emptyset\)
  • unbounded — \(\forall K\;\exists x\in S: f(x)
  • optimal solution exists — some \(x^*\!\in S\) with \(f(x^*)\le f(x)\;\forall x\in S\)
Warning for general \(f, S\): none of the three may apply cleanly — e.g. \(\min e^{x}\) over \(\mathbb{R}\) has infimum 0, never attained. Not infeasible, not unbounded, no optimal solution!
One goal of Ch. 2: prove LPs are never that pathological. For LPs the trichotomy is exact.
Reminder on conventions: we minimize (and write \(Ax\ge b\)) to match convex optimization, where minimizing convex functions is the home setting — today's geometry ports straight into that world.

Local vs Global Optima

Global: \(f(x^*)\le f(x)\) for all \(x\in S\).
Local: beats only its neighbors — all \(x\in S\) within distance \(\varepsilon\).
The three properties we will earn:
  1. Any LP is infeasible, unbounded, or has an optimal solution
  2. Every local optimum is a global optimum
  3. Nonempty polytope \(\Rightarrow\) an optimal solution at a corner
The shape property behind everything

Convex Sets

Definition. \(S\) is convex if for all \(y,z\in S\) and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\): \[\lambda y + (1-\lambda)z \in S\] — the whole segment between any two points stays inside.
  • \(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z\): a point sliding from \(z\) (\(\lambda{=}0\)) to \(y\) (\(\lambda{=}1\))
  • Intersections of convex sets are convex (check both definitions!)
  • Halfspaces are convex \(\Rightarrow\) so is every polyhedron
Convexity tester: pick a shape, then click two points inside it

Hyperplanes & Halfspaces (Def. 2.3)

For nonzero \(a\in\mathbb{R}^n\), scalar \(b\):
  • \(\{x : a'x = b\}\) — a hyperplane (line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\))
  • \(\{x : a'x \ge b\}\) — a halfspace
  • The hyperplane is the boundary of its halfspace
  • A hyperplane = intersection of two opposing halfspaces
  • \(a\) is perpendicular to the hyperplane (recall the explorer from Lecture 2!)

Polyhedra (Def. 2.1): Intersections of Halfspaces

Definition. A polyhedron is any set of the form \(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : Ax \ge b\}\) — the intersection of finitely many halfspaces.
  • The feasible set of every LP is a polyhedron
  • Standard form \(\{x : Ax = b,\, x\ge 0\}\) is a polyhedron too (equalities = pairs of inequalities)
  • As an intersection of convex sets, a polyhedron is convex
Def. 2.2: a bounded polyhedron (fits in a big box) is called a polytope.
Build one: start from the whole plane, carve with halfspaces
§2.2 · what exactly is a corner?

Three Definitions of "Corner Point"

1. Extreme point
not a convex combination of other points of \(P\)
geometric
2. Vertex
unique winner of some LP over \(P\)
geometric / optimization
3. Basic feasible solution
\(n\) linearly independent active constraints
algebraic — computable!
Punchline (Thm 2.3): all three are equivalent. We think geometrically, and compute algebraically — the best of both worlds.
Why three? Each definition makes a different theorem easy. Mathematicians hoard good definitions like tools.

Extreme Points (Def. 2.6)

Definition. \(x\in P\) is an extreme point if we cannot write \[x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z\] with \(y,z\in P\), both \(\ne x\), and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\).
  • Purely geometric — doesn't care how \(P\) is written down
  • Interior points and edge-interior points always fail: they're midpoints of neighbors
Negation practice (Lecture 3!): to show \(x\) is not extreme, exhibit \(y, z, \lambda\). To show it is, assume such \(y,z\) exist and derive a contradiction.
Click anywhere in the polygon — is it extreme?

Vertices (Def. 2.7)

Definition. \(x\in P\) is a vertex if there exists a cost vector \(c\) such that \[c'x < c'y \quad \text{for every } y\in P,\; y\ne x\] — i.e. \(x\) is the unique optimal solution of some LP over \(P\).
  • A supporting line (hyperplane) that touches \(P\) only at \(x\)
  • You verified this by hand in the Lecture-2 grapher: drag \(c\) and some corner wins uniquely

Active Constraints (Def. 2.8)

Constraint \(i\) is active (tight, binding) at \(x^*\) if it holds with equality: \(a_i'x^* = b_i\).
  • Active constraints are the ones \(x^*\) is pressed against
  • Interior point: nothing active. Edge point: one active. Corner (in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)): two active.
  • This is the bridge from geometry to algebra: corners are where enough constraints are active

Basic (Feasible) Solutions (Def. 2.9)

Definition. \(x^*\) is a basic solution if
  1. all equality constraints are active, and
  2. among all constraints active at \(x^*\), some \(n\) are linearly independent
If a basic solution also satisfies every constraint, it is a basic feasible solution (BFS).
"Linearly independent constraints" = the vectors \(a_i\) are linearly independent. \(n\) independent active constraints pin down a single point — that's what makes it algebraic and computable.

The Equivalence Theorem (Thm 2.3)

Theorem. For a nonempty polyhedron \(P\) and \(x^*\in P\), the following are equivalent: (a) \(x^*\) is a vertex  ↔  (b) \(x^*\) is an extreme point  ↔  (c) \(x^*\) is a basic feasible solution.
Why it matters:
  • Being a BFS looks representation-dependent (it mentions constraints) — the theorem says it is not: geometry decides
  • We may use whichever definition makes the current proof easiest — and we will, constantly
Proof architecture (done carefully in BT §2.2): vertex \(\Rightarrow\) extreme \(\Rightarrow\) BFS \(\Rightarrow\) vertex — a cycle of three implications gives all six. A classic trick worth stealing for your own proofs.

Setup. \(x^*\) is a vertex, so some \(c\) satisfies \(c'x^* < c'y\) for every other \(y\in P\).
"Extreme" is a cannot-be-written statement. What's the natural proof shape?
Contradiction: assume it can be written that way Induction on the dimension Find a counterexample
Assume not: \(x^*=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z\) with \(y,z\in P\), both \(\ne x^*\), \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). Since \(y,z\neq x^*\), in fact \(\lambda\in(0,1)\).
Time to deploy the vertex property. What does it say about \(c'y\) and \(c'z\)?
\(c'y > c'x^*\) and \(c'z > c'x^*\), both strictly \(c'y < c'x^*\) Nothing — \(y,z\) are arbitrary
Then \[c'x^*=\lambda\,c'y+(1-\lambda)\,c'z \;>\; \lambda\,c'x^*+(1-\lambda)\,c'x^* = c'x^*.\]
\(c'x^* > c'x^*\) is absurd \(\;\unicode{x21af}\;\). No such \(y,z,\lambda\) exist, so \(x^*\) is an extreme point. \(\blacksquare\)

We want: \(x^*\) extreme \(\Rightarrow\) \(x^*\) is a BFS. Which restatement gives us the most to work with?
The contrapositive: not a BFS \(\Rightarrow\) not extreme Work directly from the definition of extreme
Assume \(x^*\in P\) is not a BFS: among the constraints active at \(x^*\), fewer than \(n\) are linearly independent.
The active vectors \(\{a_i\}_{i\in I}\) span a subspace of dimension \(< n\). What does linear algebra hand us?
A nonzero direction \(d\) with \(a_i'd=0\) for every \(i\in I\) A direction that improves the objective Nothing useful
Take \(\varepsilon>0\) and define \(\;y=x^*+\varepsilon d,\qquad z=x^*-\varepsilon d.\)
Why are \(y\) and \(z\) still in \(P\) for small \(\varepsilon\)? Consider active and inactive constraints separately.
Active: \(a_i'y=a_i'x^*=b_i\) unchanged. Inactive: strict slack absorbs a small \(\varepsilon\) Because \(P\) is convex
For inactive \(i\): \(a_i'x^*>b_i\). Choose \(\;\varepsilon < \min\limits_{i\notin I,\,a_i'd\ne0}\dfrac{a_i'x^*-b_i}{|a_i'd|}\;\) so every inactive constraint still holds at \(y\) and \(z\). Hence \(y,z\in P\).
\(y\ne z\), both in \(P\), and \(\;x^*=\tfrac12y+\tfrac12z\). So \(x^*\) is not extreme. \(\blacksquare\)

Only Finitely Many Corners (Cor. 2.1)

Corollary. A polyhedron defined by \(m\) linear constraints in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has finitely many basic solutions — at most \(\binom{m}{n}\).
Proof idea. A basic solution is pinned by a choice of \(n\) linearly independent active constraints out of \(m\); each choice pins at most one point; there are \(\binom{m}{n}\) choices. \(\blacksquare\)
Finite \(\ne\) small! The unit cube in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (constraints \(0\le x_i\le 1\), so \(m=2n\)) has \(2^n\) BFS — you rotated that cube in Lecture 2.

Adjacent Basic Solutions & Edges

Definition. Two distinct basic solutions in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) are adjacent if they share \(n-1\) linearly independent active constraints.

If both are feasible, the segment joining them is an edge of \(P\).
  • In \(\mathbb{R}^2\): adjacent corners share one active line — the edge between them
  • Adjacency = "one constraint swap away." The simplex method walks edge by edge, exactly like the animation you saw on the grapher

Summary & What's Next

  • Polyhedron = finite intersection of halfspaces; always convex; bounded ones are polytopes
  • Corner, three ways: extreme point (geometry) = vertex (optimization) = BFS (algebra) — Thm 2.3
  • Corners are pinned by \(n\) independent active constraints \(\Rightarrow\) only finitely many (but possibly astronomically many)
  • Adjacent corners share \(n-1\) active constraints — the edges a future algorithm will walk
